Why Tank Capacity Matters

Knowing your water tank's exact capacity underpins nearly every water planning decision — from sizing a pump correctly, to scheduling irrigation cycles, to simply knowing how many days of household or livestock water you have in reserve before needing to refill. Tank capacity is printed on some commercial tanks, but many farm and household tanks are custom-built or unlabeled, making a quick, accurate calculation from basic dimensions genuinely useful.

The Two Standard Tank Volume Formulas

Cylindrical Tanks

Volume (m³) = π × (Diameter ÷ 2)² × Height
Litres = Volume (m³) × 1,000

Since most cylindrical tanks are measured by their diameter rather than radius, this calculator takes diameter directly and halves it internally before squaring.

Rectangular Tanks

Volume (m³) = Length × Width × Height
Litres = Volume (m³) × 1,000

Measuring Tips for Accuracy

  • Measure internal dimensions where possible. External measurements include wall thickness, which slightly overstates actual usable capacity.
  • For cylindrical tanks, measure diameter at multiple points if the tank isn't perfectly uniform, and use the average.
  • Measure height from the true base to the maximum fill line, not necessarily the very top rim, if there's a manufacturer-recommended maximum fill level below the brim.
  • For partially filled tanks, use the current water height instead of full tank height to estimate how much water is currently stored rather than total capacity.
This calculator provides a mathematical estimate based on the dimensions entered. Manufacturer-stated capacity, if available, should be treated as more authoritative than a dimension-based calculation, since it accounts for the tank's actual internal shape and any design features.

Sizing a Tank for Your Needs

A practical way to size a new tank is to start from your expected daily water usage and multiply it by how many days of buffer storage you want. A household using roughly 500 litres a day that wants a 4-day buffer would target around 2,000 litres of capacity, for example. Farm applications, particularly for irrigation buffering or livestock, often require substantially larger capacity and may combine multiple tanks or a farm pond alongside tank storage.

Final Thoughts

Whether you're checking an existing tank's rated capacity, sizing a new installation, or figuring out how much water you currently have in storage, converting a few simple dimensions into a reliable litre figure removes the guesswork. Bookmark this page and use it any time you're planning tank purchases, pump sizing, or water budgeting.
