3. Metric vs Imperial - Why Both Exist

Most of the world uses the metric system (meters, kilograms, liters, Celsius) because it's decimal-based - every unit scales by powers of 10, which makes conversion and calculation straightforward. The United States, and to a lesser extent the UK, still uses imperial or US customary units (feet, pounds, gallons, Fahrenheit) for everyday measurements, a holdover from British imperial standards that predate the metric system's adoption. Since neither system is going away, a reliable converter that handles both is genuinely useful - whether you're reading a US recipe in cups and ounces, following a UK road sign in miles, or converting a US product spec into centimeters and kilograms for comparison.

4. Understanding the Temperature Formulas

Temperature is the one category here that isn't a simple multiplication, because Celsius, Fahrenheit, and Kelvin don't share a common zero point. The tool uses the standard formulas:

  • Celsius to Fahrenheit: °F = (°C × 9/5) + 32
  • Fahrenheit to Celsius: °C = (°F − 32) × 5/9
  • Celsius to Kelvin: K = °C + 273.15

Kelvin is the scientific standard because it starts at absolute zero (the coldest theoretically possible temperature) rather than at an arbitrary point like water's freezing point (0°C) or a historical reference (32°F) - which is why Kelvin has no negative values in physical reality.

5. Digital Storage: Why Your Files "Shrink" When You Check File Size

Digital storage has a well-known quirk worth explaining: there are two competing definitions of "kilobyte." Computer memory and operating systems traditionally calculate in binary, where 1 KB = 1,024 bytes, 1 MB = 1,024 KB, and so on (powers of 2) - this is what this tool uses, matching how Windows, macOS, and most file managers display file sizes. Hard drive and SSD manufacturers, however, typically advertise capacity using the decimal definition, where 1 KB = 1,000 bytes (matching the metric "kilo" prefix) - which is the real reason a "1 TB" drive shows up as roughly 931 GB in your operating system. Neither number is wrong; they're just two different, both legitimate, conventions - this tool follows the binary (1,024-based) convention since that's what you'll see reflected on your own device.

6. Common Unit Conversion Mistakes

  • Confusing US and UK gallons/pints: A US gallon (3.785 L) is smaller than a UK imperial gallon (4.546 L) - always check which system a recipe or spec is using. This tool uses US customary volume units.
  • Treating "ton" as one universal unit: A US "short ton" (2,000 lb) differs from a UK "long ton" (2,240 lb) and a metric tonne (1,000 kg, ≈2,204.6 lb) - this tool uses the metric tonne.
  • Rounding too early in multi-step conversions. If you're converting through an intermediate unit, keep full precision until the final step to avoid compounding rounding errors.
  • Mixing up decimal and binary storage units, as explained above - a "GB" on a spec sheet and a "GB" shown by your OS can differ by about 7%.

7. Precision and Rounding

The converter calculates using full floating-point precision internally and displays results rounded to a readable number of decimal places (typically 4-6, depending on the category and unit). For most everyday purposes - cooking, travel, shopping, fitness - this level of precision is more than sufficient. If you need a specific number of decimal places for a scientific or engineering context, use the displayed value as your starting point and round to your own required precision.

Why does my file size look different here than on my hard drive's box? ▼
This tool uses the binary (1,024-based) definition of KB/MB/GB, matching what your operating system shows. Storage manufacturers typically advertise capacity using the decimal (1,000-based) definition, which is why the two numbers don't match.
Does this tool store the values I enter? ▼
No. All conversions happen locally in your browser using JavaScript - nothing you type is sent to or saved on Toolvala's servers.
Can I convert between categories, like weight to volume? ▼
No - weight and volume aren't directly convertible without knowing the substance's density, so each category only converts between its own units (for example, length units to other length units).
