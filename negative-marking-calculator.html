What Is Negative Marking?

Negative marking is a scoring rule used in many competitive exams, where marks are deducted for every wrong answer. If you answer a question correctly you gain marks, if you leave it blank you get zero, and if you answer it wrongly you lose a fraction of the marks. The idea is simple: an exam should measure what you know, not how lucky you are at guessing.

Without a penalty, a student who knows 60 answers and randomly guesses the other 40 on a four-option paper would collect about 10 extra marks from luck alone. With negative marking, that advantage shrinks or disappears, so the final score reflects real knowledge and good judgement. This is why the rule is common in SSC, Banking, Railways, UPSC, MPSC, NEET, JEE and many state-level exams.

The penalty is usually written as a fraction of the question's marks. You will see phrases like "one-fourth negative marking", "1/3 negative marking" or "0.25 marks deducted for each wrong answer". Some exams, such as NEET, use a fixed deduction of one mark against four marks for a correct answer. Some exams have no negative marking at all, and some apply it only to certain sections.

Negative marking changes the way you should take a test. Every attempt becomes a small decision: is my chance of being right high enough to risk the penalty? A good negative marking calculator helps you answer that question with numbers. It shows your true score after deductions, your accuracy, how many marks the penalty cost you and what accuracy you need before an attempt is worth it.

Negative Marking Formula and How It Is Calculated

The calculation has only three steps. First work out the marks you earned from correct answers. Then work out the marks deducted for wrong answers. Finally subtract the second number from the first.

Net Score = (Correct × Marks per Correct) − (Wrong × Negative Marks per Wrong)Unattempted questions are not counted in the formula because they score zero.

Here are the supporting formulas used by this calculator:

  • Attempted questions = Correct + Wrong
  • Unattempted questions = Total questions − Attempted
  • Accuracy (%) = Correct ÷ Attempted × 100
  • Attempt rate (%) = Attempted ÷ Total questions × 100
  • Maximum marks = Total questions × Marks per question
  • Percentage (%) = Net score ÷ Maximum marks × 100
  • Marks lost to negative marking = Wrong × Negative marks per wrong

Understanding the fractions

When an exam says the penalty is a fraction, multiply that fraction by the marks of one question. For a 1-mark question, 1/4 negative marking means 0.25 marks are cut. For a 2-mark question, the same 1/4 rule cuts 0.5 marks. For a 2-mark question under 1/3 negative marking, the cut is 2 ÷ 3 = 0.67 marks (more exactly 0.6667). For 1/2 negative marking on a 1-mark question the cut is 0.5. If the deduction equals the marks, one wrong answer wipes out one correct answer.

Worked Examples

These examples show how the formula works with real-looking numbers. You can type the same values into the calculator to check them.

Example 1: SSC CGL Tier 1100 questions, 2 marks each, 0.5 negative. A candidate attempts 85 questions and gets 70 correct, so 15 are wrong and 15 are unattempted.
Gross marks = 70 × 2 = 140. Penalty = 15 × 0.5 = 7.5. Net score = 140 − 7.5 = 132.5 out of 200 (66.25%). Accuracy = 70 ÷ 85 = 82.35%.
Example 2: Bank Prelims (IBPS / SBI)100 questions, 1 mark each, 0.25 negative. The candidate attempts 80, with 62 correct and 18 wrong.
Gross = 62. Penalty = 18 × 0.25 = 4.5. Net score = 62 − 4.5 = 57.5 out of 100. Accuracy = 62 ÷ 80 = 77.5%.
Example 3: UPSC Prelims GS Paper I100 questions, 2 marks each, 1/3 negative (0.6667 per wrong). The candidate attempts 80, with 52 correct and 28 wrong.
Gross = 52 × 2 = 104. Penalty = 28 × 0.6667 = 18.67. Net score = 104 − 18.67 = 85.33 out of 200 (42.67%). Notice how 28 wrong answers cost almost 19 marks, nearly as many as nine correct answers would earn.
Example 4: NEET UG180 questions, 4 marks each, 1 mark negative. The candidate gets 140 correct, 25 wrong and leaves 15 blank.
Gross = 140 × 4 = 560. Penalty = 25 × 1 = 25. Net score = 560 − 25 = 535 out of 720. Each wrong answer costs one mark in addition to the four marks that were not earned, so a wrong answer is effectively a five-mark swing compared with a correct one.

The pattern in these examples is that a small number of wrong answers does limited damage, but a large number of careless attempts can erase a big share of your gains. That is why accuracy matters at least as much as the number of questions attempted.

Break-Even Accuracy: When Is Guessing Worth It?

Break-even accuracy is the chance of being right at which an attempt neither adds nor removes marks on average. It is calculated as:

Break-even = Negative marks ÷ (Marks per correct + Negative marks)If your chance of being right is above this number, attempting adds marks on average. Below it, you lose marks on average.

For 1/4 negative marking on a 1-mark question, break-even is 0.25 ÷ 1.25 = 20%. For 1/3 negative marking it is 25%, for 1/2 it is 33.3%, and when the penalty equals the marks it is 50%.

What this means on a four-option question

A pure guess on a four-option question gives a 25% chance of being right. Under 1/4 negative marking that is above the 20% break-even point, so a blind guess is worth a tiny positive amount, about +0.06 marks per 1-mark question on average. Under 1/3 negative marking a pure guess is exactly break-even (25%), so it neither helps nor hurts on average. Under 1/2 negative marking a pure guess loses about 0.125 marks per question on average.

Things change quickly once you can remove options. If you can eliminate one option, your chance rises to about 33%. If you can eliminate two, it becomes 50%. Under 1/4 negative marking those are clearly profitable attempts, and under 1/3 negative marking they are profitable too. Under 1/2 negative marking, eliminating one option brings you to break-even and eliminating two makes the attempt worthwhile.

Important: these figures describe averages over many questions. On any single question you may still lose marks. Also, "chance of being right" must be honest. Feeling confident is not the same as being accurate. Use mock tests to find out how reliable your gut feeling really is.

10 Strategies to Reduce Negative Marking

  1. Know the scheme before you start. The penalty and the break-even accuracy decide how bold you can be. Note them on your rough sheet.
  2. Attempt in two rounds. In round one answer only the questions you are sure about. In round two come back to the doubtful ones with the time left.
  3. Use elimination. Cross out options that are clearly wrong. Each option removed raises your chance of being right and makes an attempt safer.
  4. Set a confidence rule. For example, attempt only when you are at least 50% sure under a 1/3 or 1/2 scheme. Keep the rule simple so you can follow it under pressure.
  5. Watch the wording. Words like "not", "except", "incorrect" and "which of the following is false" cause many wrong answers. Underline them as you read.
  6. Do not change answers without a reason. Change an answer only if you find a clear mistake or new information, not because of a vague doubt.
  7. Practise with timed mocks and track accuracy. Write down your attempts, correct answers and wrong answers after each mock and run them through this calculator to spot trends.
  8. Analyse every wrong answer. Sort errors into knowledge gaps, silly mistakes and guesses. Silly mistakes are the cheapest to fix.
  9. Balance speed and accuracy. Attempting more questions only helps if your accuracy stays high. A small drop in accuracy can cancel the gain from extra attempts.
  10. Stay calm near the end. Many candidates panic and guess wildly in the last minutes. Decide your final-minute policy in advance, based on break-even accuracy.

Common Mistakes in Negative Marking Calculations

  • Applying the fraction to total marks. The deduction is a fraction of the marks of one question, not of the whole paper.
  • Counting unattempted questions as wrong. In most exams a blank answer scores zero, so it should not be penalised in your calculation.
  • Rounding too early. With 1/3 negative marking, using 0.33 instead of 0.6667 on a 2-mark question can shift your total by several marks over many wrong answers. Keep full precision and round only the final result.
  • Mixing sections. If sections have different marks or penalties, calculate each section separately and add the net scores.
  • Ignoring special rules. Some exams treat multiple answers as wrong, some give no penalty on numerical questions and some have sectional cut-offs. Read the instructions.
  • Using the answer key without checking the version. Final keys can differ from provisional keys, so recalculate when the key changes.
  • Forgetting normalisation. In exams held in multiple shifts, your raw score may be adjusted. A calculator gives the raw score only.

Plan Your Target Score with the Planner

A target score turns into a clear plan once you know your accuracy. The planner on this page uses the formula: marks per attempted question on average = accuracy × (marks + penalty) − penalty. Dividing your target by that value tells you how many questions you need to attempt.

For example, in SSC CGL Tier 1 (2 marks, 0.5 penalty) at 80% accuracy, each attempt is worth 0.8 × 2.5 − 0.5 = 1.5 marks on average. To reach 120 marks you need about 80 attempts. At 90% accuracy each attempt is worth 1.75 marks, so only about 69 attempts are needed. A ten-point rise in accuracy saves you around eleven attempts, which is a lot of time and stress.

The planner also warns you when a target cannot be reached at your current accuracy even if you attempt every question, and it tells you the accuracy you would need in that case. Use it after each mock test to set a realistic goal for the next one.

Using the calculator after every mock test

The best way to use this tool is as part of a regular routine. After each mock test, enter your numbers, note your net score and accuracy, and write down the number of wrong answers. Over four or five mocks you will see a pattern. If your accuracy is steady but your score is flat, you may need to attempt a few more questions. If your attempts are rising but your accuracy is falling, the penalty is eating your gains and you should slow down. Tracking these two numbers together is far more useful than looking at the raw score alone, and it makes your preparation measurable instead of a guess.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

1. How do you calculate negative marking?

Multiply the correct answers by the marks per question, multiply the wrong answers by the negative marks per wrong answer, and subtract the second result from the first. Unattempted questions score zero.

2. What is 1/4 negative marking?

It means one-fourth of the marks of a question are deducted for each wrong answer. That is 0.25 for a 1-mark question and 0.5 for a 2-mark question.

3. What is 1/3 negative marking?

One-third of the marks of the question are deducted for each wrong answer. For a 2-mark question the deduction is about 0.67 marks.

4. Is it better to leave a question blank or guess?

Compare your chance of being right with the break-even accuracy. If your chance is higher, attempting adds marks on average. If it is lower, leaving the question blank is the better choice.

5. Are unattempted questions penalised?

In almost all exams they are not. They simply score zero. Check the official instructions for your exam to be sure.

6. Can my final score be negative?

Yes, in theory. If your wrong answers cost more marks than your correct answers earn, the net score is below zero. The calculator shows such a result in red.

7. What accuracy should I aim for?

A good target is 85% or more on the questions you attempt. The break-even accuracy is only the minimum. Higher accuracy lets you attempt more questions safely.

8. Does this calculator work for sectional negative marking?

Yes. Calculate each section on its own with its own marks and penalty, then add the net scores.

9. Why does my score differ from the official result?

Reasons include a change in the answer key, normalisation across shifts, questions that were dropped or given to all candidates, and special rules in the exam. This calculator gives a raw score based on the numbers you enter.

10. Is my data stored?

No. All calculations happen in your browser and nothing you type is uploaded or saved.

11. Can I use it for school or college tests?

Yes. Choose Custom, enter the total questions, marks and penalty, and use it for any objective test.

12. How do I find my accuracy?

Divide the number of correct answers by the number of questions you attempted and multiply by 100. The calculator shows this automatically.
