Understanding Irrigation Water Requirement

Every irrigation decision ultimately comes down to one core question: how much water does the field actually need, and for how long should the source run to deliver it? The relationship connecting these is surprisingly simple once you know one key conversion — a single millimetre of water depth spread evenly over one square metre of land equals exactly one litre. This single fact is the foundation of nearly every irrigation water calculation used in agriculture, from small farm ponds to large canal-fed schemes.

This calculator uses that relationship to turn a recommended irrigation depth (in millimetres) and your field area into a total water volume, expressed in litres, cubic metres, and the traditional hectare-centimetre unit still widely used in Indian irrigation planning — and, if you know your pump or source's flow rate, straight into a practical run-time in hours or minutes.

The Core Irrigation Formula

Water Volume (litres) = Irrigation Depth (mm) × Field Area (m²)

Since one hectare equals 10,000 square metres, a simpler working rule follows directly: one millimetre of irrigation depth over one hectare equals exactly 10,000 litres, or 10 cubic metres. So a 50mm irrigation over a 2-hectare field requires 50 × 10,000 × 2 = 1,000,000 litres, or 1,000 cubic metres.

Converting to Hectare-Centimetres

The hectare-centimetre (ha-cm) is a traditional Indian irrigation unit representing one centimetre of water depth over one hectare — equal to 100,000 litres or 100 cubic metres. It remains common in canal water allocation and irrigation department planning documents.

1 hectare-cm = 100,000 litres = 100 m³

Calculating Irrigation Duration

Once you know the total volume needed, dividing it by your water source's flow rate gives the time required to deliver it:

Duration (hours) = Total Volume (litres) ÷ Flow Rate (litres/hour)

Crop Water Requirement vs Irrigation Water Requirement

It's worth distinguishing two related but different concepts. Crop water requirement is the total water a crop needs across its growth cycle, drawn from rainfall, stored soil moisture, and irrigation combined. Irrigation water requirement is specifically the artificial portion that must be supplied through irrigation, calculated after subtracting effective rainfall and any usable soil moisture contribution from the crop's total need. This calculator computes the volume for whatever depth figure you enter — whether that's a full crop water requirement or just the irrigation portion is determined by the number you input.

Factors That Affect Real-World Irrigation Efficiency

  • Conveyance losses: Water travelling through unlined earthen channels can lose a meaningful share to seepage before it ever reaches the field, particularly over longer distances.
  • Application method: Flood or furrow irrigation typically has lower application efficiency than sprinkler systems, which in turn are typically less efficient than drip irrigation.
  • Soil type: Sandy soils drain faster and often need more frequent, lighter irrigations, while clay soils hold water longer but can be prone to waterlogging if over-irrigated.
  • Evaporation and wind: Hot, windy conditions increase water loss during application, particularly with sprinkler systems, and may justify a slightly larger calculated volume.
This calculator computes a gross water volume based on the depth and area you enter, for general planning purposes only. It does not account for site-specific conveyance losses, soil infiltration rates, or crop growth stage adjustments — consult your local irrigation department or an agricultural engineer for precise scheduling.

Tips for Efficient Irrigation Water Use

  • Irrigate based on crop growth stage. Water needs are typically highest during flowering and grain-fill or fruiting stages, and lower during early establishment and near maturity.
  • Check soil moisture before irrigating. A simple hand-feel test or soil moisture meter can prevent both under- and over-irrigation.
  • Irrigate during cooler parts of the day where possible, to reduce evaporation losses, particularly with sprinkler systems.
  • Maintain channels and pipes. Reducing seepage and leakage in conveyance systems directly reduces the total water you need to pump or draw.
  • Consider drip irrigation for high-value crops. Its higher application efficiency can meaningfully reduce total water volume needed compared to flood irrigation.

Final Thoughts
